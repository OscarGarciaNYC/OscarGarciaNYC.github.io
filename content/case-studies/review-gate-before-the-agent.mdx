## Executive summary

TreeTales is a gift-first Memory Book: several people write short stories about
one person, and those stories become a printed and digital keepsake. I own it
end to end, product and architecture and code. Working with coding agents,
production outpaced my ability to read it almost immediately, which made my own
review capacity the binding constraint on the project. Every governance decision
follows from treating that capacity as a hard number. So the delivery system
came first: separate architect, builder, and reviewer agents, no agent permitted
to both write and approve, all output landing as draft pull requests, and me as
the only merge, which caps overnight throughput at my morning reading speed
deliberately and has never once been the thing that slowed the product down.

## Product problem and stakes

Group gifts fail in a specific and predictable way. The organizer is
enthusiastic, the contributors are willing, and the thing still collapses,
because contributing is unbounded work with no obvious finish line. "Write
something about Mom" is a request for an essay, a commitment, and a judgment
about your own writing, all at once. Most people quietly opt out, and the
organizer ends up writing five entries themselves and calling it a book.

So the product problem is making a contribution feel finishable. That reframe
determines nearly everything downstream: how an invitation reads, how much
scaffolding a prompt provides, whether an account is required before writing,
and the one that turned out to matter most, what an AI is allowed to do to
someone's words.

The stakes are unusual for a consumer product, because the artifact is a gift
about a real person, often an older relative, sometimes assembled after a
diagnosis. A generic paragraph in a book like that reads as a small betrayal,
and it is obvious to the recipient.

### What discovery actually changed

I went into this believing the product was a curated artifact: good photographs
and a well-shaped body of writing, assembled on someone's behalf. That belief
survived about as long as it took to talk to people. Over roughly 150
conversations — colleagues, members of my church, and a fair number of
strangers who were willing to humor a question in public — the thing people
kept asking for was the chance to write their own note in their own words. A
friend, a pastor's wife, wanted her own voice in the book and wanted her
friends' voices in it too, which is a different product from the one I had
been designing.

That correction is where the rest of the design comes from. If what a reader
values is recognizing the people who wrote to them, then the unit of value is
a voice and not a page, so a thin book carrying six contributors beats a longer
one assembled from two, and any feature that averages the prose damages the
book even where it improves the individual sentence. It is also what made the
AI guardrail obvious instead of principled, because a ghostwriting feature
would have polished every entry while erasing the reason someone opens the
book at all.

The fourth assumption is also where the competitive argument lives. Storyworth
runs a prompt-a-week model, and the complaint I heard from people who had tried
it was that they never finished, because the writing burden is long, relentless,
and lands on the recipient, who ends up authoring their own gift. Opening
contribution to a group spreads that burden across the people who wanted to
write anyway, and it shortens what any one person has to produce.

Voice is the assumption I am most confident about and least in a hurry to build.
My background is in voice AI, so I can see the shape of it clearly enough to
know that transcription across multiple languages is doable and that it is not
a first or second iteration of anything.

## Constraints and stakeholders

Three constraints shaped the build, and only one of them is technical.

**A contributor's willingness expires in minutes.** Someone opens an invitation
on a phone, between other things. Any friction placed before the writing, like
account creation or email verification or a password, spends goodwill that does
not come back. That pushed identity toward anonymous-first: you write, and the
question of who you are is settled afterward if it needs to be settled at all.

**The recipient never sees the software.** They receive a book. That sounds
obvious, and it quietly kills a whole class of engagement features, because the
person the product exists for cannot be retained, notified, or re-engaged.

**I am the only reviewer.** No second engineer, no on-call rotation, no
teammate who notices that a change looks wrong. With agents producing work
around the clock, this is the constraint that actually governs the system.

<Details summary="Why review capacity is a hard constraint">

The tempting framing is that review is a bottleneck to be widened later: hire,
or trust the agents more as they improve. Both fail here.

Hiring doesn't apply. This is a solo product by choice, and the economics only
work at a five-dependency runtime and near-zero fixed cost. Trusting the agents
more fails differently. The reviewer agent is good at finding defects in a diff
and structurally incapable of noticing that a change is the wrong thing to
build. That judgment is the part I'm not delegating, and it requires reading.

So review capacity sits at roughly an hour a day. Treating it as a budget
changes what you build: you stop optimizing for how much work the agents can
produce and start optimizing for how cheaply a human can decide whether to
accept it.

</Details>

### How I sequenced it

The order was set by which questions could be answered by talking and which
ones required building. Who the buyer is, and what they wanted the book to
contain, cost nothing but conversations, so those went first and they are the
two that changed the product. Whether a printed artifact could reach revenue
faster than the digital platform I would rather be building was a judgment
call about my own time, and I made it in favor of the thing a customer can
hold.

Only after those were settled did the constraint move from the product to me.
That is when the delivery system got built, because governing a product that
might still change shape is expensive and premature.

<Details summary="What I deferred, and why voice is still on the roadmap">

Voice recording is the clearest example of a validated idea that should wait.
People genuinely want their own voice preserved, and my background in voice AI
means I can scope it without guessing: transcription through an API service,
multiple languages, and the storage and playback questions that follow.

None of that is hard in a way that worries me, but all of it is work that does
not change whether the core loop succeeds. A contributor who will not write a
paragraph is unlikely to record one either, so voice earns its place once the
writing experience is unambiguously working.

</Details>

### What this unlocked, and what it foreclosed

What it unlocked is the reason to do any of this: the delivery system is the
durable asset. Because roles are separated and decisions are written down,
adding a capability is mostly a matter of describing it well. Feature work that
would normally justify a small team is tractable alone, which is what makes a
five-dependency runtime and near-zero fixed cost sustainable.

The cost of that arrives the day a second person does. Every governance rule in
the system assumes one human holding merge authority, so a collaborator would
need their own review lane, their own dispatch budget, and a trust model that
currently exists nowhere except in my head and thirteen decision records. I
built this knowing it would be excellent for one person and genuinely awkward
for two, and that hiring anyone would put the whole governance layer back on
the drawing board before they could ship a line.

## Technical architecture or implementation considerations

The delivery system and the product share one principle: make the expensive
thing structurally impossible.

In delivery, that is separation of duties. In the product, it is
**anonymous-first identity with row-level security in Postgres**. A contributor
writes before any account exists. That's a product decision, and it's the
difference between a contribution that happens and one that doesn't, but it
means authorization can never depend on the application remembering to check.
The database enforces who can read a story, so a mistake in application code
cannot leak someone's writing about their mother.

The guardrail that most defines the product is a constraint on the model:
**AI proofreads, never ghostwrites.** It fixes spelling, punctuation, and
obvious grammar, and it leaves phrasing, length, and rhythm alone. This is the
rule I have argued about most, and it survives those arguments because the
failure mode never announces itself: nobody writes in to complain that their
paragraph came back better than they wrote it, and the damage only shows up in
a finished book where every contributor sounds faintly the same.

<Details summary="Where the guardrail actually lives">

Stating a guardrail in a prompt makes it a preference. Three things make it a
property of the system:

1. The proofreading path is a separate, narrowly scoped call with its own
   instructions. It never shares a context window with any generative feature,
   so there is no path by which "improve this" leaks into "correct this."
2. The output is diffed against the input, and a change beyond a bounded edit
   distance is rejected. A rewrite fails closed.
3. The contributor sees the correction and can decline it. The last word on
   someone's own sentence belongs to them.

The third is the only one a contributor ever notices, and putting the final
decision back in their hands is what keeps the first two from being a promise I
make to myself.

</Details>

Beyond that, the deliberate choices are unglamorous and mostly about staying
cheap to change: a five-dependency runtime, a print-on-demand pipeline so no
inventory exists, and a glossary that fixes what words like *story*,
*contributor*, and *book* mean, so that agents and I are not quietly building
against different definitions.

Three things I would carry to any project where agents do the building.

**Review capacity is the real capacity.** It is tempting to measure an agentic
setup by how much it can produce, but optimizing that number actively makes
things worse, because unreviewed output is a liability that compounds while it
waits; the number worth measuring is how cheaply a human can decide to accept
something, and the system should be designed toward that instead.

**Separation of duties earns its cost.** The single most useful thing I built
was an agent whose only job is to object, which works precisely because it has
not just spent an hour assembling the change and convincing itself the approach
was correct.

**Write the guardrail into the product promise.** "AI proofreads, never
ghostwrites" sits in the marketing copy where a contributor can hold me to it,
which is why it survived the three or four occasions I wanted to build
something that would have quietly broken it. I would treat any responsible-AI
commitment that has never once cost its author a feature they wanted as a
statement of intent that has not been tested yet.
