## Executive summary

TreeTales is a gift-first Memory Book: several people write short stories about
one person, and those stories become a printed and digital keepsake. I own it
end to end — product, architecture, and the code — which meant the interesting
constraint was never how fast software could be produced. Working with coding
agents, production outpaced my ability to read it within the first week. The
binding constraint was my own review capacity, and every governance decision on
the project follows from treating that as a hard number rather than a
temporary inconvenience. So the delivery system came first: separate architect,
builder, and reviewer agents, no agent permitted to both write and approve, all
output landing as draft pull requests, and me as the only merge. That caps
overnight throughput at my morning reading speed on purpose. It is live, and
the cap has never been the thing that hurt.

## Product problem and stakes

Group gifts fail in a specific and predictable way. The organizer is
enthusiastic, the contributors are willing, and the thing still collapses,
because contributing is unbounded work with no obvious finish line. "Write
something about Mom" is a request for an essay, a commitment, and a judgment
about your own writing, all at once. Most people quietly opt out, and the
organizer ends up writing five entries themselves and calling it a book.

So the product problem is not collection or printing. It is making a
contribution feel finishable. That reframe determines nearly everything
downstream: how an invitation reads, how much scaffolding a prompt provides,
whether an account is required before writing, and — the one that turned out to
matter most — what an AI is allowed to do to someone's words.

The stakes are unusual for a consumer product, because the artifact is a gift
about a real person, often an older relative, sometimes assembled after a
diagnosis. A generic paragraph in a book like that is not a quality problem. It
is a small betrayal, and it is obvious to the recipient.

<Constraint>
The product's core promise is that the recipient reads their people's actual
voices. Any feature that improves prose quality by making the voices more
similar is disqualified, however much it improves the average sentence.
</Constraint>

## Constraints and stakeholders

Three constraints shaped the build, and only one of them is technical.

**A contributor's willingness expires in minutes.** Someone opens an invitation
on a phone, between other things. Any friction placed before the writing —
account creation, email verification, a password — spends goodwill that does
not come back. That pushed identity toward anonymous-first: you write, and the
question of who you are is settled afterward if it needs to be settled at all.

**The recipient is not a user and never sees the software.** They receive a
book. That sounds obvious, and it quietly kills a whole class of engagement
features, because the person the product exists for cannot be retained,
notified, or re-engaged.

**I am the only reviewer.** No second engineer, no on-call rotation, no
teammate who notices that a change looks wrong. With agents producing work
around the clock, this is the constraint that actually governs the system.

<Details summary="Why review capacity is a hard constraint and not a staffing problem">

The tempting framing is that review is a bottleneck to be widened later — hire,
or trust the agents more as they improve. Both fail here.

Hiring does not apply: this is a solo product by choice, and the economics only
work at a five-dependency runtime and near-zero fixed cost. Trusting the agents
more fails differently. The reviewer agent is good at finding defects in a diff
and structurally incapable of noticing that a change is *not what the product
should do* — that judgment is the part I am not delegating, and it is the part
that requires reading.

So review capacity is roughly fixed at an hour a day. Treating it as a budget
rather than a bottleneck changes what you build: you stop optimizing for how
much work the agents can produce and start optimizing for how cheaply a human
can decide whether to accept it.

</Details>

### What this unlocked, and what it foreclosed

What it unlocked is the reason to do any of this: the delivery system, not the
product, is the durable asset. Because roles are separated and decisions are
written down, adding a capability is mostly a matter of describing it well.
Feature work that would normally justify a small team is tractable alone,
which is what makes a five-dependency runtime and near-zero fixed cost
sustainable rather than a temporary state before hiring.

What it forecloses is worth naming, because it is permanent. This architecture
cannot absorb a second person cheaply. Every governance rule assumes exactly one
human with merge authority; adding a collaborator means rewriting the trust
model, not adding a seat. I chose a system that is excellent for one and
awkward for two, deliberately — but if TreeTales ever needs a team, that
decision gets paid for in full.

## Technical architecture or implementation considerations

The delivery system and the product share one principle: make the expensive
thing structurally impossible rather than discouraged.

In delivery, that is separation of duties. In the product, it is
**anonymous-first identity with row-level security in Postgres**. A contributor
writes before any account exists. That is a product decision — it is the
difference between a contribution that happens and one that doesn't — but it
means authorization can never be a matter of the application remembering to
check. The database enforces who can read a story, so a mistake in application
code cannot leak someone's writing about their mother.

The guardrail that most defines the product is a constraint on the model:
**AI proofreads, never ghostwrites.** It fixes spelling, punctuation, and
obvious grammar. It does not improve phrasing, does not lengthen, does not
smooth. This is the rule that gets argued with most, and it is not negotiable,
because the failure mode is invisible: nobody complains that their paragraph
came back better, and the recipient reads a book where everyone sounds faintly
the same.

<Details summary="Implementation notes — where the guardrail actually lives">

Stating a guardrail in a prompt makes it a preference. Three things make it a
property of the system:

1. The proofreading path is a separate, narrowly scoped call with its own
   instructions. It never shares a context window with any generative feature,
   so there is no path by which "improve this" leaks into "correct this."
2. The output is diffed against the input, and a change beyond a bounded edit
   distance is rejected rather than surfaced. A rewrite fails closed.
3. The contributor sees the correction and can decline it. The last word on
   someone's own sentence belongs to them.

The third one is the only one a user ever notices, and it is the one that makes
the other two honest.

</Details>

Beyond that, the deliberate choices are unglamorous and mostly about staying
cheap to change: a five-dependency runtime, a print-on-demand pipeline so no
inventory exists, and a glossary that fixes what words like *story*,
*contributor*, and *book* mean, so that agents and I are not quietly building
against different definitions.

Three things I would carry to any project where agents do the building.

**Review capacity is the real capacity.** It is tempting to measure an agentic
setup by how much it can produce. That number is not the constraint and
optimizing it makes things worse, because unreviewed output is a liability that
compounds. Measure how cheaply a human can decide to accept something, and
design toward that.

**Separation of duties is not ceremony.** The single most useful thing I built
was an agent whose only job is to object. Not because it is smarter than the
builder, but because it has not just spent an hour convincing itself the
approach was correct.

**Write the guardrail into the product promise, not the launch post.** "AI
proofreads, never ghostwrites" is a constraint I can be held to, and it has
killed features I wanted. A responsible-AI principle that has never cost you a
feature is not a principle yet.
