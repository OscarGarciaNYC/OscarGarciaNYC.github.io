I work where enterprise data, AI, and the systems of record meet — usually on the
unglamorous half of the problem. Most enterprise AI programs I've seen fail on
readiness rather than on models: the data doesn't support the use case, nobody
owns the decision the model is supposed to inform, or the thing works in a demo
and dies in the security review. That's the part I spend my time on.

Most recently I've been the right hand to a Chief Digital & Information Officer,
which in practice means translating between two groups who both think they're
being clear: executives describing outcomes, and engineers describing systems. I
built an agentic AI roadmap from nothing, which mostly meant building a way to
say no — an ROI-ranked pipeline that killed low-readiness use cases before
engineering committed to them. Killing things well turns out to be most of the
job.

I also build. I design and operate model-agnostic multi-agent workflows and ship
real production software with them, which is the only reason I trust my own
opinions about agents. Writing a roadmap for a technology you've never operated
produces confident, wrong documents.

<h2 id="disclosure">How I handle confidentiality</h2>

Case studies drawn from client and employer work carry this line at the top:
*this case study generalizes details and omits confidential information while
preserving the underlying product problem, constraints, and decision process.*

That's a real constraint, not a formality, and it shapes what you'll find here.
Account names, internal scale, and system specifics are generalized. The problem,
the constraints, the alternatives, and the reasoning are not — those are the
parts worth reading anyway, and they're the parts that transfer to your situation
rather than describing mine. Work on my own products is exact and checkable, and
says so.

What I never do is generalize my own role. Where a piece says I advised, I
advised. A generalized claim that quietly upgrades what I did would be worse than
a named one, because you couldn't check it.

## How I work

**Kill use cases before engineering commits to them.** A prioritized pipeline is
only worth having if things actually come off it. The expensive failure isn't
picking the wrong use case, it's discovering it was wrong after four engineers
spent a quarter on it.

**Name the constraint, not the win.** Every system I've shipped was shaped by
something I couldn't do — a close date that wouldn't move, a security review that
wouldn't bend, a review capacity I couldn't expand. The constraint is the
interesting part, and it's the part that transfers.

**Autonomy is a budget, not a default.** On my own product, agents can produce
work faster than I can read it. So the review gate came first, the dispatch
budget is hard, everything lands as a draft, and I am the only merge. That's not
caution about AI; it's arithmetic.

**Say what a thing costs.** An architecture decision without a stated cost isn't
a decision, it's an advertisement. Every architecture piece on this site names
what I gave up, because a tradeoff I can't articulate is one I didn't make on
purpose.
