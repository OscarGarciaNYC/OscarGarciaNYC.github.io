I work where enterprise data, AI, and the systems of record meet, usually on the
unglamorous half of the problem. Most enterprise AI programs I've seen fail on
readiness. The data doesn't support the use case, nobody owns the decision the
model is supposed to inform, or the thing works in a demo and dies in the
security review. That's the part I spend my time on.

Most recently I've been the right hand to a Chief Digital & Information Officer,
which in practice means translating between two groups who both think they're
being clear: executives describing outcomes, and engineers describing systems. I
built an agentic AI roadmap from nothing, which mostly meant building a way to
say no. An ROI-ranked pipeline that killed low-readiness use cases before
engineering committed to them. Killing things well turns out to be most of the
job.

I also build. I design and operate model-agnostic multi-agent workflows and ship
real production software with them, which is the only reason I trust my own
opinions about agents. Writing a roadmap for a technology you've never operated
produces confident, wrong documents.

<h2 id="disclosure">How I handle confidentiality</h2>

Case studies drawn from client and employer work carry this line at the top:
*this case study generalizes details and omits confidential information while
preserving the underlying product problem, constraints, and decision process.*

That constraint shapes what you'll find here. Account names, internal scale, and
system specifics are generalized. The problem, the constraints, the
alternatives, and the reasoning stay intact, because those are the parts worth
reading and the parts that transfer to your own situation. Work on my own
products is exact and checkable, and says so.

I never generalize my own role. Where a piece says I advised, I advised. A
generalized claim that quietly upgrades what I did would be worse than a named
one, because you couldn't check it.

## How I work

**Kill use cases before engineering commits to them.** A prioritized pipeline is
only worth having if things actually come off it. The expensive failure is
discovering a use case was wrong after four engineers spent a quarter on it.

**Name the constraint.** Every system I've shipped was shaped by something I
couldn't do: a close date that wouldn't move, a security review that wouldn't
bend, a review capacity I couldn't expand. The constraint is the interesting
part, and it's the part that transfers.

**Treat autonomy as a budget.** On my own product, agents can produce work
faster than I can read it. So the review gate came first, the dispatch budget is
hard, everything lands as a draft, and I am the only merge. The cap is
arithmetic.

**Say what a thing costs.** An architecture decision without a stated cost is an
advertisement. Every architecture piece on this site names what I gave up,
because a tradeoff I can't articulate is one I didn't make on purpose.
