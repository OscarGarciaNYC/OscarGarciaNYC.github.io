I work where enterprise data, AI, and the systems of record meet, usually on
the unglamorous half of the problem. Most enterprise AI programs I have seen
fail on readiness long before they fail on capability: the data does not
support the use case, nobody owns the decision the model is supposed to inform,
or the thing works in a demo and then dies in the security review. That last
category is where I spend most of my time.

Most recently I have been the right hand to a Chief Digital & Information
Officer, which in practice means translating between two groups who both
believe they are being perfectly clear — executives describing outcomes and
engineers describing systems. I built an agentic AI roadmap from nothing, which
mostly meant building a way to say no: an ROI-ranked pipeline whose value came
almost entirely from the use cases it killed before engineering had committed
anything to them.

I also build, which is the only reason I trust my own opinions about agents. I
design and operate model-agnostic multi-agent workflows and ship real
production software with them, having learned that writing a roadmap for a
technology you have never operated yourself produces documents that are
confident and wrong in ways nobody catches until delivery.

That constraint shapes what you will find here. Account names, internal scale,
and system specifics are generalized, while the problem, the constraints, the
alternatives I considered, and the reasoning stay intact, because those are the
parts that transfer to a situation of your own. Work on my own products is
exact and checkable, and says as much on the page.

The one thing I never generalize is my own role, so where a piece says I
advised, I advised; a generalized claim that quietly upgraded what I did would
be worse than a named one you could look up, because you would have no way to
test it.

## How I work

**Kill use cases before engineering commits to them.** A prioritized pipeline
is only worth maintaining if things actually come off it, and the failure that
costs real money is the one you find a quarter in, when four engineers have
already built against an assumption about the data that nobody checked at the
start.

**Name the constraint.** Every system I have shipped was shaped by something I
could not do: a finance close that fell on the third business day and would not
move, a security review that had to pass before a renewal cleared, a review
capacity of about an hour a day. Those are the details somebody with a
different problem can actually use, and they are usually the first thing a
case study leaves out.

**Treat autonomy as a budget.** On my own product, agents produce work faster
than I can read it, so the review gate was designed before the agents were, the
dispatch budget stops the night when it runs out, and everything waits as a
draft until I merge it. What looks like a governance philosophy is mostly an
honest estimate of how many diffs I can read before losing the thread.

**Say what a thing costs.** Every architecture piece on this site names what
the decision gave up, usually in a column next to what it bought, because the
version without that column is a sales page. When I cannot fill the column in,
it generally turns out I inherited the decision from somebody else and never
examined it.

and video against a fixed Sunday deadline with a volunteer crew who are doing
me a favor. It has taught me more about rollout planning than most
